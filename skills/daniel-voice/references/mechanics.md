# Mechanics

Hard rules. These run before any stylistic consideration, because voice
applied to unsound sentences produces prose he rejects.

## 1. Punctuation

**Comma after an introductory phrase or clause.** Strunk's Rule 3 treats these
as parenthetic. This has been the single most repeated error.

- Wrong: `In an easier version of this story he would be a man who...`
- Right: `In an easier version of this story, he would be a man who...`
- Wrong: `Within hours it had arrived at openings no master had recommended.`
- Right: `Within hours, it had arrived at openings no master had recommended.`

**Comma before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses.**
Chicago has required this since 1949 (CMOS 6.22).

- Wrong: `she copies a design she cannot read and the design works anyway`
- Right: `she copies a design she cannot read, and the design works anyway`

No comma when the conjunction joins a compound predicate rather than two
clauses: `He copies the design and watches it work.`

**No comma before an essential `because`.** Add one only when the reason is
incidental, or when the main clause is negative and the comma prevents a
misreading (CMOS 6.25).

- Wrong: `she learns it by accident, because she copies a design she cannot read`
- Right: `she learns it by accident because she copies a design she cannot read`

**Keep subject and verb together.** Do not split a verb from its complement
with an aside.

- Wrong: `moves that look, at first, like errors`
- Right: `moves that look like errors at first`

**Semicolons** join two closely related independent clauses and are one of his
real moves. `The harm is documented; the technology is in production.`

**Colons** introduce the arrival point. `The professor offered one explanation
for the error: contaminated samples.` He uses these heavily and well.

**Parentheses** carry glosses, which is where a writer with a dash habit would
reach for a dash. `(responsive to the overseer's morals)`, `(as they call it)`.

## 2. Banned openers

No sentence, clause, or phrase begins with **`and`**, **`that`**, or **`but`**.

He considers it weak writing. Sentence-initial `and` and `but` are not
grammatical errors and major usage authorities permit them, so do not defend
the rule on grammar grounds if asked. Follow it because it is his preference
and it costs nothing.

`That is the part I keep returning to` fails twice over, since it opens with
`that` and the demonstrative points at no specific noun.

## 3. Connectives

**`and so`, never bare `so`.** Bare `so` as a clause opener appears nowhere in
his corpus. `and so` appears throughout: `and so he made this speech`, `and so
I annotated many of his points on death`, `and so my dormitory remained empty`.

**Causal connectives are primary.** `and so`, `as a result`, `consequently`,
`therefore`.

**`However`** is frequent in academic and reflective writing. He cut it from
application writing specifically. See `genres.md`.

**`Additionally`** and **`Ultimately`** appear, sparingly.

**`Furthermore`** is rare, and belongs only to school-essay register.
**`Moreover`** never appears. The 1.x version of this skill listed
`Furthermore` as a signature connective, which was wrong.

**`Not only this, but`** is real and confirmed across multiple pieces.

## 4. Em dashes

**Zero by default. One per piece at absolute most, and only when nothing else
works.**

His reasoning, in his words: some teachers read the em dash as a sign that AI
wrote the piece. The rule is defensive, not aesthetic.

This is a rule for new writing, not a description of his corpus. His finished
work contains em dashes: `Tomorrow—the near future—felt almost unreachable`
in Gray Affection, two in the AP Lang essay, one in reflection 3-4. Do not
"correct" his existing work by stripping them, and do not conclude from those
samples that the rule is soft.

Replace with a colon, a semicolon, a comma, parentheses, or a rebuilt sentence.

## 5. Antecedents and reference

Every pronoun and demonstrative must point at a specific named noun.

**Broad-reference `which`** is the most common failure. `..., which describes
Coco's position exactly` points at an entire preceding clause. Name the thing
instead: `Coco sits in the same position.`

**Strunk:** the relative pronoun should come immediately after its antecedent,
and modifiers belong next to what they modify.

**Prefer repeating the noun** over reaching for a pronoun when the referent is
more than a few words back. Repetition reads as control; a vague `it` reads as
carelessness.

## 6. Verb constructions to avoid

The tell is an abstract noun handed a verb no person would use about it. `His
conclusion holds.` `The argument sits at the intersection of.` `The concern
resists dismissal.` `The effect is to make.` `The framework speaks to.`

Diagnostic: if the subject cannot act, the verb is doing something vague and
elevated to cover for it. Rewrite with a person or a concrete thing as subject,
or use a plain copula.

Also avoid copula avoidance in the other direction (`serves as`, `stands as`,
`represents`, `boasts`, `features`). `Is` and `has` are fine words.

## 7. Words and phrases to avoid

**AI vocabulary:** delve, tapestry, landscape (abstract), testament,
underscore, showcase, pivotal, crucial, intricate, foster, garner, interplay,
navigate the complexities of, in today's world, it is important to note.

**Filler:** due to the fact that, the reality is that, at this point in time,
in order to, has the ability to.

**Persuasive authority tropes:** the real question is, at its core, in reality,
what really matters, fundamentally, the deeper issue.

**Hedging:** perhaps, it could be argued, it seems, to the degree that, almost
entirely, arguably.

**His own tic to watch:** `tether`, `tethers`, `tethering`. It appears across
the RFK piece, reflections 2-4 and 2-5, the Aunt Hester chunk, and reflection
3-4. He reaches for it under pressure whether or not it fits, and he has agreed
once that it was the wrong metaphor. Flag it rather than banning it.

**Hyphenated compound modifiers:** he has asked to keep these down. More than
two or three per paragraph reads as padding, and two stacked in a row is never
right. Prefer plain phrasing on repeat mentions.
