## 1. How flow actually works

From Gopen and Swan, *The Science of Scientific Writing*:

> Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the
> stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize.

The **topic position** is the opening of a sentence. Readers expect a sentence
to be a story about whoever shows up first, so the opening should carry
something the previous sentence already established.

The **stress position** is the end, at the moment of syntactic closure, where
readers naturally assign the most emphasis. Save the payload for it.

**Disjointedness is cold openings.** A paragraph reads as choppy when sentences
begin on names or abstractions the paragraph has not introduced yet. This is
the actual mechanism, and it is not fixed by lengthening sentences or adding
transition words.

Diagnostic pass: read only the first four or five words of each sentence in
order. If that sequence tells a connected story, the paragraph flows. If any
opening arrives cold, rebuild it.

Worked example. Every opening picks up the previous sentence:

> Every chess engine built before 2017 learned the game from **people who
> already knew how to play it**. **Programmers** encoded what masters had
> concluded over a century of tournament practice... **Those engines** could
> search far deeper than a human, yet the standard they searched toward was a
> human standard... AlphaZero began outside **those bounds**...

## 2. Weighting a sentence

Before building a sentence, decide two things: what it is about, and what is
new about it. The first goes first. The second goes last.

A weak ending wastes the most emphatic slot in the sentence. `Buntarō climbs
alone, and the manga never lets the reader settle on what the solitude is for`
closes on `for`, a preposition, in the stress position. `Buntarō climbs alone
for a reason Nitta never supplies` closes on `never supplies`.

Gopen's related rules:

- Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb.
- Articulate the action of every clause in its verb.
- A sentence is too long when it has more candidates for the stress position
  than there are stress positions available. This is what makes a forced
  tricolon fail: three items competing for one slot.

## 3. The LL framework

From his AP Lang class. This is the vocabulary he was graded in, and it is
more precise than anything derived from the corpus alone.

**LL1 — Strong main clauses.** The most important idea goes in the main clause.
Use strong precise verbs, cut filler, avoid weak `is`/`are` constructions
carrying real content. `What remains is choice` should be `Removing incapacity
leaves only choice`.

**LL2 — Coordination and subordination.** Coordination implies the two ideas
carry equal weight. Subordination states an actual relationship: cause, time,
concession, condition. Choose based on the real relationship, never on how long
the sentence comes out. `Nina finished editing the article, and she realized
the introduction still wasn't strong enough` becomes `After finishing the
edits, Nina realized the introduction still wasn't strong enough.`

**LL3 — Adjectivals.** Participial phrases, relative clauses, and infinitives
of purpose fold description into a sentence while the main clause keeps the
main action. `A crowd hoping to see the author arrive formed around the main
entrance.`

**LL4 — Nominals.** Appositives and concept nouns compress. `The keynote
speaker, a climate scientist who has spent years studying glacier melt
patterns, addressed the audience.`

**LL5 — Parallel structure.** Match the grammatical form across a series.
Gerunds with gerunds, infinitives with infinitives.

**LL6 — Purposeful punctuation.** Semicolon for closely related independent
clauses, colon to spotlight an arrival point.

Note: the class materials teach the dash as an LL6 move, and his teacher's own
suggested revisions use it. His em dash rule overrides that for anything he
submits. The two are in tension and he knows it.

## 4. Varying the joint

Repeating one joining structure is what he calls chopped. The problem is not
sentence length; six long sentences built identically read worse than a mix.

The specific pattern to avoid is `independent clause, and independent clause`
repeated down a paragraph.

Available structures, to rotate through:

- Simple subject-verb-object
- Concessive opener (`Although X, Y`)
- Participial opener (`Drawing the ascents almost wordlessly, Nitta...`)
- Participial or appositive tail
- Relative clause embedding
- Colon elaboration
- Semicolon join
- Gerund or nominal subject
- Conditional (`If X, then Y`)
- Prepositional opener

Rule of thumb: no two consecutive sentences share a joining structure.

## 5. Stuffing

The opposite failure, and just as bad. Cramming two ideas that each deserve a
main clause into one sentence demotes one of them, usually the payload.

`Nitta forecloses that explanation early, showing Buntarō speak easily enough
to the people around him and return to the rock regardless` buries the return
to the rock, which is the paragraph's actual turn, inside a participial phrase.

Test: if a clause carries the point of the paragraph, it belongs in a main
clause. Combine only when one idea genuinely serves the other.

## 6. Rhythm and ratios

**Long comma-managed sentences are the baseline.** His texture is subordination
and embedding, held together with semicolons and colons.

**Short sentences are rare accents.** At most one per paragraph, placed after a
long one, carrying a real turn rather than a punchline. `The measure is the
problem.` `Two states at once.` A run of short sentences reads as manufactured
drama.

**Tricolons and contrast pairs are real** and land at paragraph ends. `The
harm is documented; the technology is in production; the audits are produced by
the companies that built the tools.` Do not force them, and never let three
items compete for one stress position mid-paragraph.

**Corrective redefinition** is his signature move: state what a thing is not,
then what it is. `Not a failure of discipline, but a measurement.` `Not a
discovery, but rather a withheld confirmation.` Once or twice per piece, at a
paragraph landing. Four times in six paragraphs is parody.

**Escalating repetition** appears at emotional peaks. `He is the object. He is
everything.` `You are nothing, and yet you are everything all at once.` Rare,
and only where the content earns it.

**X-yet-Y** is his core sentence shape under pressure. `The most powerful of
all, yet the most useless.` It is the compressed form of concede-then-push.
